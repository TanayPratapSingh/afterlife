Dear Editors,

Please consider "Asking Retraction Watch Twice: Retraction Data Revise Their
Own Past" for publication as an Article in Quantitative Science Studies.

Since Crossref opened the Retraction Watch database in 2023, many studies of
retraction have relied on it, yet it is republished daily and revised as it
goes. I replayed 39 snapshots of its public history and asked each the same
questions about retractions dated 2023 or earlier. Between September 2024 and
October 2026 their number grew 9.1% and those citing "Paper Mill" 80.6%,
mostly through late cataloguing of Hindawi retractions, while a renamed reason
made one common query return zero. The paper also compares four retraction
registries, shows from OpenAlex's public code why half of its retracted works
are retraction notices, and reports a blind hand audit estimating that 95% of
flags held by a single registry are real retractions.

The work extends the retraction indexing studies of Schneider, Salami, and
colleagues, and answers the suggestion of Si, Salami, and Schneider (2026) to
assess retraction metadata over time. It ends with six practices for making
retraction studies repeatable, starting with citing and archiving the
snapshot used.

The manuscript is posted as a preprint on MetaArXiv (https://doi.org/10.31222/osf.io/9wgpy_v1). All source data
are archived with checksums at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/D7GSF, and the
code and hand labels are at https://github.com/TanayPratapSingh/afterlife. The
manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, I have no competing
interests, and the work had no specific funding. The use of generative AI
tools is disclosed in the manuscript.
