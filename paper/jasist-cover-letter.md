Dear Editors,

Please consider "Asking Retraction Watch Twice: Retraction Data Revise Their
Own Past" for publication as a research article in the Journal of the
Association for Information Science and Technology.

The paper is about the quality and stability of a dataset that much of the
field now relies on. Since Crossref opened the Retraction Watch database in
2023, studies of retraction have cited it as if it were one fixed source, yet
it is republished daily and revised as it goes. I replayed 39 snapshots of
its public history and asked each the same questions about retractions dated
2023 or earlier. Between September 2024 and October 2026 their number grew
9.1% and those citing "Paper Mill" 80.6%, mostly through late cataloguing of
Hindawi retractions, while a renamed reason made one common query return
zero. The paper also compares four retraction registries, shows from
OpenAlex's public code why half of its retracted works are retraction
notices, and reports a blind hand audit estimating that 95% of flags held by
a single registry are real retractions.

For JASIST readers who build or use bibliographic infrastructure, the paper
ends with six practices for making retraction studies repeatable, starting
with citing and archiving the snapshot used.

The manuscript is posted as a preprint on MetaArXiv
(https://doi.org/10.31222/osf.io/9wgpy_v1). All source data are archived with
checksums at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/D7GSF, and the code and hand
labels are at https://github.com/TanayPratapSingh/afterlife. The manuscript
