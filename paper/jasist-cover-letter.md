Dear Editors,

Please consider "Asking Retraction Watch Twice: Retraction Data Revise Their
Own Past" for publication as a research article in the Journal of the
Association for Information Science and Technology.

The paper builds on information science research into the quality of
retraction metadata. Schneider et al. (2023) and Salami, McCumber, and
Schneider (2024) showed that bibliographic databases disagree about which
papers are retracted. Ortega and Delgado-Quirós (2024) compared how seven
databases index retracted literature, and Hauschke and Nazarovets (2025)
found OpenAlex flagging corrections and expressions of concern as
retractions. Si, Salami, and Schneider (2026) concluded that "retraction
metadata are dynamic rather than static" and named longitudinal assessment
as future work. This paper supplies that assessment for the Retraction Watch
database. I replayed 39 snapshots of its public history and asked each the
same questions about retractions dated 2023 or earlier. Between September
2024 and October 2026 their number grew 9.1% and those citing "Paper Mill"
80.6%, mostly through late cataloguing of Hindawi retractions. Records citing
"Fake Peer Review" fell from 4,654 to zero when the label was renamed.

It draws on two fields outside information science. From data warehousing it
takes the practice of keeping every version of every record with a validity
interval, so the database can be queried as it stood on any date. From
biomedical research integrity it adds PubMed and PubMed Central to the
comparison, as Sebo and Sebo (2025) did for PubMed in medicine. Joining these
with OpenAlex, I found that two in five of OpenAlex's retracted works are
retraction notices and traced the cause to a rule in its public ingest code;
OpenAlex has since confirmed the problem and logged it for a fix. I also
hand audited a blind, stratified sample of papers flagged by only one
registry. An estimated 97% are real retractions, so most disagreement is a
registry missing a retraction.

For JASIST readers who build or use bibliographic infrastructure, the paper
treats a reference database as versioned data and ends with six practices for
making retraction studies repeatable, starting with citing and archiving the
snapshot a study used.
